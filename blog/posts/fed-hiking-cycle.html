The Fed Is Playing a Game Nobody Has the Rules For

Everyone's waiting for the pivot. But what if the pivot isn't coming? A look at what a structurally higher rate environment means for your portfolio — and why the 2010s playbook is dead.

🧙
151
Independent research · Published May 20, 2025

There's a particular kind of investor — and I've been guilty of this — who watches each Fed meeting the way a kid watches the door on Christmas morning. Every press conference is a potential pivot. Every softening word from Jerome Powell is a green light. The rate cut is always six months away.

We've been doing this for two years. It's time to consider the possibility that we're wrong about the fundamental question: not when rates come down, but whether the post-2008 rate environment was the anomaly — and this is the norm.

The zero-rate decade was not normal

From 2009 to 2022, U.S. interest rates spent most of their time near zero. This was a deliberate, unprecedented policy choice in response to the financial crisis and, later, a pandemic. It shaped an entire generation of investment frameworks:

  • Growth stocks with no earnings were worth it — free money had no opportunity cost
  • Real estate was a one-way trade — financing was permanently cheap
  • "TINA" (There Is No Alternative) made equities the only game in town
  • The 60/40 portfolio worked because bonds went up when stocks went down

Every single one of these dynamics either broke or severely weakened in 2022. And yet an enormous amount of portfolio thinking still implicitly assumes we're going back to zero. We might not be.

Key Takeaway The 10-year Treasury has averaged around 5.5% over the past century. The post-2008 period of near-zero rates was 14 years of historical exception. We may simply be reverting to the long-run mean.

What's actually keeping rates high

The standard narrative goes: inflation comes down → Fed cuts → we're back to normal. But there are three structural forces that weren't present in 2010 that could keep rates elevated regardless of where inflation ends up:

1. The fiscal situation. The U.S. government is running a 6–7% deficit in a non-recession year. That's historically only happened during wars or financial crises. The Treasury has to fund this deficit by issuing bonds, which puts constant upward pressure on yields. At some point, the bond market starts demanding a premium just to absorb the supply.

2. Demographics and deglobalization. The disinflationary forces of the last three decades — cheap Chinese manufacturing, demographic dividend of baby boomers in peak production years, cheap global supply chains — are reversing. Labor is getting more expensive. Supply chains are being reshored. Neither of these is transitory.

3. The energy transition. The green transition is inherently inflationary in the short to medium term. Building out a new energy infrastructure — solar, wind, grid storage, EVs — requires enormous capital expenditure and has real commodity intensity. That's demand pressure on industrial inputs that wasn't there before.

What this means for portfolios

I want to be honest here: I don't know where rates end up. Nobody does. But the risk of being wrong in one direction is much larger than the other for most portfolios. If rates fall back to zero, your tech stocks do fine. If they stay at 4–5% for the next decade, a lot of "investments" are actually very expensive assets with poor forward returns.

Here's how I'm thinking about it:

Position Sizing Logic If you believe there's even a 30–40% chance rates are structurally higher for a decade, that should show up in your portfolio. Not a dramatic repositioning, but an honest acknowledgment that the 2010s playbook deserves less weight in your framework than it currently probably gets.

Concretely, that means: being more selective in high-duration assets (long-dated growth stocks, long-term bonds), paying more attention to free cash flow yield rather than narrative, taking real assets seriously (energy, commodities, real estate with strong cash flows), and being honest about the math on leveraged structures.

The 2010s playbook is dead

This doesn't mean equities are bad. It doesn't mean you should stuff cash under your mattress. It means the particular variety of "just buy the index and everything compounds because rates are zero and the Fed will always save you" investing deserves a harder look.

The markets of the 2020s and 2030s will probably reward different things than the markets of the 2010s. Pricing power. Real assets. Cash generation. Businesses that don't need cheap financing to work. International diversification away from a market trading at 20x earnings when risk-free rates are 5%.

None of this is exciting. But the boring thesis — that markets revert to something resembling historical norms — is sometimes the most powerful one.
